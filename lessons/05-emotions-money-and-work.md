# Lesson 5: Naming emotions, and handling money and work stress
<!-- facts: Crisis and help lines; Calming the mind; When it may be more than stress -->

## By the end, the learner can

- Notice and name an emotion, and explain why naming it helps them choose what to do next.
- Explain the difference between feeling an emotion and acting on it.
- Say why money and work are common causes of stress, and where to learn more about each.
- Use one skill from lessons 3 and 4 on a money or work worry.

## Key ideas

**Emotions are information, not instructions.** Anger, fear, sadness and frustration all tell you
something matters. You can feel them without having to act on them straight away.

**Notice, name, choose.** When a strong feeling arrives: notice it in your body (tight chest, hot
face), name it ("I'm feeling anxious", "I'm angry"), then choose what to do. Naming puts a little
space between the feeling and your reaction. WHO's free guide (`facts.md`) has related skills:
unhooking from difficult thoughts, and making room for emotions.

**Read the room too.** The same skill works on other people: "she seems frustrated" helps you
respond to what they feel, not only to what they said. Being aware of feelings, yours and
theirs, is what people mean by emotional intelligence.

**Money stress.** Debt and bills are a common cause of long-lasting stress, because they don't go
away by themselves. The worry tree (lesson 4) helps: most money problems have a first step. The
course **south-african-money** covers budgeting and debt, if it's available.

**Work stress.** Too much work, unclear expectations or conflict with a manager can wear you down.
Work stress that goes on without being managed is what WHO calls burnout (lesson 2). Knowing your
rights helps; the course **south-african-rights-at-work** covers them for South Africa, if it's
available. Other countries' courses may come later.

## Worked example

Thabo's manager criticises his report in front of the team. He feels his face go hot and wants to
snap back. He notices it, names it ("I'm angry and embarrassed"), takes a few slow breaths, and
says he'll come back with changes. Later, in his worry time, he plans what to ask his manager about
what was wrong.

## Exercise

Describe three made-up situations (a rude taxi driver, a bank SMS about a missed payment, a friend
cancelling plans) and ask the learner to name the feeling each might bring, and one helpful thing
to do next.
