# Lesson 6: Supporting a friend who is struggling
<!-- facts: Crisis and help lines; Supporting someone -->

## By the end, the learner can

- Name warning signs that someone may be struggling.
- Start a caring conversation and listen without trying to fix everything.
- Ask directly about suicide, and know why asking is safe.
- Say what to do if a friend is in danger, and why they shouldn't carry it alone.

## Key ideas

**Notice the signs.** Changes in mood, sleep, eating or routine; pulling away from people;
drinking more; talking about feeling hopeless, worthless or trapped (`facts.md`). Not everyone
shows signs, so a check-in is never wasted.

**Start simply, somewhere private.** "You've seemed quiet lately. How are you really doing?" Then
listen. Open questions ("what's been happening?") help more than advice. You don't need the right
words; showing you care is the point.

**Listen, don't fix.** Let them talk. Don't judge, compare it with your own problems or rush to
solutions. Saying back what you heard ("it sounds like work has been really hard") shows you're
listening.

**Ask about suicide directly** if you're worried: "Are you thinking about suicide?" Research shows
asking does not put the idea in someone's head or make it more likely (`facts.md`). It gives them
permission to tell you.

**If they say yes, or you think they're in danger:** stay with them, help them call or message a
crisis line (`facts.md`), and if a life is at risk call the emergency number. Don't promise to
keep it secret. Then follow up in the days after.

**Look after yourself too.** Supporting someone is hard. Tell someone you trust, and you can call
the same help lines for advice about a friend.

## Worked example

Naledi notices her friend Zinhle has stopped coming to choir and isn't answering messages. She
visits and asks how she's really doing. Zinhle says she feels like a burden. Naledi asks her
directly whether she's thinking about suicide. Zinhle says sometimes. Naledi stays with her, they
call a crisis line together, and Naledi checks in again the next day.

## Exercise

Ask the learner to write the first sentence they'd say to a friend they're worried about, and an
open question to follow it. Then ask: what would you do if the friend said they'd thought about
ending their life?
